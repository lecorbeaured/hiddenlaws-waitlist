Rich Dad Poor Dad vs The Psychology of Money: Which Should You Read?

These two get compared constantly because they're often read back to back, but they're not really competing for the same job. Rich Dad Poor Dad is a framework book: it gives you a lens (assets vs. liabilities) and tells you to act on it. The Psychology of Money is a behavior book: it explains why people who know exactly what they should do with money still don't do it.

What Rich Dad Poor Dad actually teaches

Kiyosaki's core claim is simple and sticky: the poor and middle class buy liabilities they think are assets, the rich buy assets that generate cash flow. It's taught through parables — his "rich dad" and "poor dad" — rather than data, which is exactly why it's so repeatable and exactly why critics push back on it. The book is a mindset shift, not a step-by-step plan.

What The Psychology of Money actually teaches

Morgan Housel isn't arguing with the asset/liability framework — he's one level underneath it, asking why smart people with good information still behave irrationally with money. His answer: money decisions are driven by ego, fear, and the specific story your own history taught you about risk, far more than by spreadsheets. It's less "here's what to do" and more "here's why you won't do it, and how to notice that in yourself."

Here's the gap neither book closes: Kiyosaki tells you to buy assets. Housel tells you why you might sabotage that even when you know better. Neither one explains why some people never had to fight that battle in the first place — why the "rich dad" instinct was just normal in their house growing up, and never had to be learned from a book at all.

That's the specific question The Hidden Laws of Money is built around: ten unspoken rules about money that get passed down in some households as a given, and the rest of us never get handed. It's less a competing framework and more the missing layer underneath both of these books — paired with a 36-page workbook so the insight turns into action inside 72 hours, before it fades the way most "aha" moments from a book do.
